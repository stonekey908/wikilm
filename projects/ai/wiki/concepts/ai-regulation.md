# AI Regulation

The evolving legal and policy frameworks governing AI development and deployment, characterized in 2026 by fragmentation between federal, state, and international approaches.

## US Landscape (2026)
**Federal level**: Trump administration pursuing deregulation — executive orders preempting state laws, discouraging new federal legislation. AI companies deploy super-PACs to influence Congress.

**State level**: Active and accelerating.
- **California SB 53**: Requires frontier AI labs to publish safety testing results; whistleblower protections for safety researchers
- **New York RAISE Act**: Safety protocols and incident reporting requirements
- **150 AI bills** passed by US states in 2025 — record high
- Congress failed twice to pass a moratorium blocking state laws

**Key tension**: Federal preemption vs. state experimentation. AI companies prefer a single federal standard (more predictable, likely weaker). States argue they must fill the vacuum.

## EU Landscape (2026)
- EU AI Act prohibitions on predictive policing and emotion recognition took effect
- Risk-based tiered framework: unacceptable risk, high risk, limited risk, minimal risk
- Applies to any model deployed in EU — covers US and Chinese providers
- Most comprehensive binding AI regulation globally as of 2026

## Transparency Crisis
Major AI companies (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic) withhold training data details, limiting:
- Independent safety research
- Regulatory oversight
- Benchmark validity (models may be trained on test data)

[[stanford-hai]] 2026 index identifies this as a critical governance gap.

## Legal Liability (Emerging Frontier)
Beyond regulation, courts are developing AI liability doctrine:
- Chatbot harmful advice → product liability?
- AI-generated defamation → platform liability or creator liability?
- Insurance industry pullback may force the issue (see [[ai-legal-liability]])
